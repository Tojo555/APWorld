Causation Toolkit

Read this once, then go practice. For every sentence, ask: what is this sentence’s job in the paragraph?

Cause

Why something happened. A paragraph can give you more than one cause of the same thing.

Effect

What happened because of a cause. One cause can have several effects.

Main effect

The big result the whole paragraph is explaining. When a paragraph lists several causes, they usually all lead to one main effect.

Root cause

The first cause in a chain. It starts the other causes in motion.

Plague kills workers
root cause→ Labor shortage
cause→ Higher wages
main effect

Neither

Some sentences give background or description. They are neither a cause nor an effect: where something is, what something was, or a fact that doesn’t explain why anything happened.

Two quick tests. Cause test: can you put “because of this…” in front of the next event? Effect test: can you put “as a result…” in front of it? If neither fits, the sentence is probably background.

Historical Reasoning Identification

Every AP essay prompt and short-answer question asks for one of three kinds of thinking: causation, comparison, or continuity and change. Some items below are written like essay (LEQ) prompts and some like short-answer (SAQ) parts. Read each one, decide which kind it is, then see which words gave it away. The items start easier and generally get harder. To show mastery, get FIVE in a row right.

Quick guide to the three types
Look at the structure, not just the keywords. One event and its results is causation. Two different subjects in the same period is comparison. One subject followed across a long span of time is continuity and change.

Causation

The essay explains why something happened or what it led to. Words like caused, led to, shaped, accounts for, or made possible often appear, but the structure is what counts.

Comparison

The essay lines up two or more different subjects side by side. Words like compare, same, alike, than, or like often appear, but the prompt may never say “compare.”

Continuity and change

The essay follows one subject across a long span of time, often running from one AP period into the next (for example, c. 1200 to c. 1750). Words like changed, remained, persisted, shifted, or transformed often appear, and the prompt may never say “continuity and change.”
